03 — The axes

Five axes, and every one of them does something you can see at reading size. Nothing here is a slider for its own sake.

wght300 → 1000

Thin enough to disappear at 8pt, heavy enough to be a hoarding.

The full range is drawn, not interpolated from two masters at the ends. The 300 and the 1000 were both cut by hand and the middle was fitted to them.

CASL0 → 1

At one, the letters loosen and stop behaving in public.

Casual redraws the terminals and opens the apertures. It is not a weight and it is not an italic — it is the difference between a sign painted by a company and a sign painted by the owner.

slnt0 → −15

A true slant, not an oblique applied by the renderer.

Every glyph is corrected at the extremes: the o narrows, the diagonals are redrawn, the s is rebalanced. Software shearing gets none of that and it shows on the round letters.

MONO0 → 1

01234 · lIl1 · O0 — the labels on this page are MONO 1.

Duplexed to a fixed advance without collapsing the design. This is why the page needs no second family: the eyebrows, the numbers and the axis readouts are all Baldio with one axis pushed over.

CRSV0 → 1

The a, the e, the g and the l change their minds entirely.

Cursive swaps in single-storey forms. Set it to 0.5 and the renderer picks per glyph, which is either a feature or a small act of chaos depending on the sentence.

Specimen text

Baldio is Portuguese for a vacant lot — the piece of ground between two buildings that nobody has decided about yet. We drew it for those. Municipal notices, fly-posted gig listings, the hand-painted sign on a garage that has been a garage for forty years.

It began as a commission for a Lisbon council that wanted one face for street signage, tax letters and the website, and could not afford three. That constraint is the whole design. Five axes because five roles, and not one axis more.

Two years, one designer, and about nine hundred glyphs. Latin Extended, full European coverage, and the Portuguese ordinals nobody else bothers to draw.
